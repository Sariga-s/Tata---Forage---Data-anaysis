Before creating the analysis, I first imported the retail data and reviewed its structure and quality.

The dataset contained transaction-level information, including invoice number, product code, product description, quantity, invoice date, unit price, customer ID, and country.

I then cleaned and validated the data to make sure the analysis was reliable. I checked the data types, particularly for invoice date, quantity, and unit price. I also reviewed missing customer IDs, blank product descriptions, duplicate records, and invalid transactions.

Cancelled invoices and returned products were identified because they contained negative quantities or invoice numbers beginning with the letter C. These transactions were excluded from the sales analysis so that returns would not inflate or incorrectly reduce product demand.

I also excluded records with zero or negative unit prices. Blank customer IDs were removed specifically from the customer analysis because these transactions could not be connected to an identifiable customer.

Finally, I calculated revenue by multiplying the quantity sold by the unit price. After completing these checks, I built the dashboard and validated the totals before interpreting the results.

Question 1: Revenue trend and seasonality

The first question concerns the monthly revenue trend during 2011.

The analysis shows that revenue was relatively stable during the first half of the year. From around September, however, revenue began to increase, with the strongest performance occurring during the final quarter.

This indicates a clear seasonal pattern. The increase may be connected to customers purchasing products in preparation for Christmas and the holiday season.

For the CEO, this finding is valuable for forecasting. If a similar pattern continues, the business should prepare for higher demand before the final quarter. Inventory purchasing, warehouse capacity, staffing, marketing campaigns, and delivery operations should therefore be planned in advance.

I would also recommend investigating the products and customers responsible for the largest revenue peaks. This will help determine whether these increases came from general seasonal demand or from a small number of large orders.

Question 2: International market performance

The second analysis compares the top 10 revenue-generating countries outside the United Kingdom.

The Netherlands is the strongest international market, followed by EIRE and Germany. France and Australia also make significant contributions.

Revenue is considered alongside quantity because revenue alone does not fully explain market behaviour. For example, high revenue with a relatively lower quantity may indicate demand for premium or higher-priced products. High quantity with lower revenue may indicate a lower-priced product mix or a more price-sensitive market.

From a marketing perspective, the Netherlands, EIRE, Germany, and France should receive priority for targeted campaigns and customer-development activities.

However, before investing further, the company should also consider shipping costs, profitability, local competition, and the number of customers generating that revenue.

Question 3: High-value customers

The third visual ranks the top 10 customers by revenue in descending order.

Customer 14646 is the highest revenue-generating customer, followed by customers 18102 and 17450.

This indicates that a relatively small number of customers contribute a substantial amount of revenue. These customers should be protected through personalised communication, loyalty benefits, priority customer support, and relevant product recommendations.

At the same time, this concentration presents a potential risk. Losing one of the leading customers could have a noticeable effect on revenue. I therefore recommend not only retaining the top customers but also identifying medium-value customers with the potential to move into the high-value segment.

Question 4: Geographical demand

The final visual shows product demand by country, excluding the United Kingdom.

The map allows us to view the geographical distribution of demand on a single screen. Europe represents the strongest international demand cluster, while demand is also visible in markets such as Australia, Japan, Canada, Brazil, and South Africa.

The Netherlands, EIRE, Germany, and France appear to offer the strongest immediate opportunities because they already demonstrate meaningful revenue and demand.

Markets outside Europe may represent longer-term opportunities, but expansion decisions should not be based on demand alone. They should also consider delivery costs, market size, customer concentration, regulations, and potential profitability.

Conclusion

To conclude, the analysis provides four main recommendations.

First, prepare inventory, staffing, and marketing activities before the final-quarter increase in demand.

Second, prioritise the strongest international markets, particularly the Netherlands, EIRE, Germany, and France.

Third, protect high-value customers through targeted retention programmes while developing the next group of potential high-value customers.

Finally, conduct a profitability and operational feasibility assessment before expanding into new geographical markets.

Overall, this dashboard identifies when customers are buying, where revenue and demand are coming from, who the most valuable customers are, and where future growth opportunities may exist.
